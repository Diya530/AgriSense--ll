# Irrigation & Water Management

## General Principles

Over-irrigation is as harmful as under-irrigation: it can cause root oxygen
starvation, nutrient leaching (especially nitrogen), and increased fungal
disease risk. The right irrigation timing depends on crop growth stage, soil
type (sandy soils drain and dry faster than clay soils), and current/forecast
weather — irrigating right before expected rain is usually unnecessary.

## Critical Growth Stages for Water

Most crops have 1-2 "critical" stages where water stress causes the largest
yield loss — commonly flowering and grain/fruit filling for cereals, and
tuber bulking for potato. Water stress during vegetative growth generally
causes less yield damage than stress during these critical stages, so
irrigation scheduling that prioritizes critical stages (when water is
limited) is a widely recommended strategy.

## Practical Signs of Irrigation Need

- Soil moisture noticeably dry 5-10 cm below the surface.
- Leaves showing slight wilting during the hottest part of the day but
  recovering by evening (early stress sign) versus not recovering (more
  urgent).
- No meaningful rain expected in the next 1-2 days based on a real forecast.

## Water-Saving Techniques

- Mulching reduces surface evaporation and can meaningfully extend the
  interval between irrigations.
- Drip irrigation delivers water directly to the root zone with much less
  loss than flood irrigation, though it has higher upfront setup cost.
- Alternate wetting and drying (for rice) can reduce water use substantially
  with minimal yield impact when managed correctly.

## Spraying & Weather

Pesticide/fungicide spraying is generally not recommended shortly before
expected rain (the spray can wash off before it takes effect) or in high wind
(spray drift, uneven coverage, safety risk). Early morning or late afternoon
with low wind is commonly recommended over midday heat, which can also reduce
chemical effectiveness and increase phytotoxicity risk for some products.
