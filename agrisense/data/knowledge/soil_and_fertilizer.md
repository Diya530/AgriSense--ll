# Soil Health & Fertilizer Guidance

## Understanding NPK

Nitrogen (N) drives vegetative growth (leaves, stems) — deficiency typically
shows as yellowing of older leaves first. Phosphorus (P) supports root
development and flowering/fruiting — deficiency often shows as stunted growth
or purplish leaf discoloration. Potassium (K) supports overall plant vigor,
disease resistance, and fruit/grain quality — deficiency often shows as leaf
edge browning ("scorching").

## Soil pH

Most common crops grow best in slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 6.0-7.5),
though there are exceptions (e.g., potato and tea prefer more acidic soil).
Very acidic soils (pH below 5.5) can lock up phosphorus and micronutrients
like aluminum toxicity risk; very alkaline soils (pH above 8.5) can cause iron
and zinc deficiencies. Agricultural lime is commonly used to raise pH; gypsum
or organic matter (compost, farmyard manure) is commonly used to help lower it
gradually and improve overall soil structure.

## Common Fertilizers Used in India

- Urea: a concentrated nitrogen source, generally applied in split doses to
  reduce loss through volatilization/leaching.
- DAP (Di-Ammonium Phosphate): supplies both nitrogen and phosphorus, commonly
  applied at or before sowing.
- MOP (Muriate of Potash): the common potassium source.
- SSP (Single Super Phosphate): a phosphorus source that also supplies some
  sulfur and calcium.
- Balanced/complex fertilizers (e.g., NPK 19:19:19): convenient but should
  still be guided by an actual soil test where possible, rather than a fixed
  recipe every season.

## Organic & Micronutrient Considerations

Regular use of organic matter (compost, farmyard manure, green manure)
improves soil structure, water retention, and microbial activity over time,
and can reduce dependence on chemical fertilizer over several seasons.
Micronutrient deficiencies (zinc, boron, iron) are increasingly common in
intensively cultivated soils and are easy to miss without a soil test, since
symptoms can resemble other nutrient or water-stress issues.

## When to Get a Professional Soil Test

A basic pH/NPK reading (like the ones used in this app) is useful for
directional guidance, but a full laboratory soil test (available through most
state agricultural department soil testing labs, often free or low-cost) gives
a much more complete and locally calibrated picture, especially for
micronutrients and organic carbon content.
